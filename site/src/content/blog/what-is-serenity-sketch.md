Two years into building Serenity Sketch, and I still don't have a clean way to describe it.

That's not false modesty. It's the actual, ongoing problem. Every time someone asks "so what's the game about," I watch myself reach for a category and come up short. It doesn't sit neatly inside "game." It doesn't sit neatly inside "self-care app" either. So here's my best attempt to explain it properly.

## Not quite a self-care app

Most self-care apps open with a question. "Feeling stressed?" "Ready to try meditation?" They assume you've arrived already convinced you need fixing, and they get straight to work on you.

Serenity Sketch doesn't do that. There's no assessment, no mood check-in, no "your streak is at risk." You open it, and you paint. The self-care part isn't a wrapper around the experience. It's a side effect of it.

It's also nothing like Headspace or Calm to actually use. There's no lying down, no eyes-closed narration for twenty minutes. You're holding a stylus (or your finger), tracing water onto stone, watching the light catch it for a second before it's gone. It's active. It's a little bit playful. Which brings up the other problem.

## Not quite a game either

If Serenity Sketch were a normal game, I could point you to a Steam page and let genre tags do the talking. "Cozy," "puzzle," "walking simulator adjacent." You'd know roughly what you were getting before you opened it.

But there's no win state here. No score, no fail condition, nothing to optimize. You can't lose at painting water on a rock. The closest thing to a mechanic is a Practice: a short guided sequence where a gentle voice walks you through a painting prompt, then asks you a quiet question about what came up while you were doing it. That's not really a game loop. It's closer to a ritual with really good production values.

## So what is it

Here's the actual mechanic, stripped down: you trace water onto stone, in a natural setting, and you watch each stroke evaporate. That's it. That's the whole verb of the game.

The evaporating part matters more than it sounds like it should. When your work is going to disappear in a few seconds no matter what, something in your brain stops trying to make it good. You stop protecting the painting. You just paint. A guide talks you through it, not to instruct you, but to keep you company while you do.

After a few strokes, the Practice closes with a short reflection. Not a lesson, just a question. Sometimes you have an answer. Sometimes you don't, and that's fine too.

## The self-care app that plays like a game

That's the tagline we landed on, after a lot of worse ones. It's not a clever wordplay so much as the most honest sentence we could write. It's a self-care app in what it does for you. It plays like a game in how it feels to use.

Most self-care apps live in your head. This one lives in your hands.
